# Unexpected Verification Codes

You are sitting at home.

You are not logging into anything.

Then your phone suddenly receives:

> Your verification code is: `483921`

What does that mean?

---

## First: do not panic

An unexpected code does **not always mean your account has been hacked**.

It could be:

- someone entering your phone number by mistake
- an old device trying to sign in
- an app reconnecting
- someone attempting to access your account

But it is worth paying attention.

---

## The important question

### Did you request the code?

If the answer is:

## No

then:

- do not enter the code anywhere
- do not approve a login
- do not send the code to anyone
- do not click links in unexpected messages

<div className="alert alert--warning" role="alert">

**A verification code is only for the login you started yourself.**

If you did not start the login, do not approve it.

</div>

---

## Why would someone ask for the code?

Imagine a criminal already knows your password.

They try to log in.

The website asks them for a verification code.

But the code is sent to **your phone**.

Now the attacker has a problem.

They need you to give them the code.

---

## So they may contact you

You might receive a phone call or message saying:

> "I'm calling from your bank. We need to verify your identity."

or:

> "We've just sent you a security code. Please read it back to me."

That code may actually be the final thing they need to access your account.

<div className="alert alert--warning" role="alert">

**Never give an unexpected login or verification code to somebody who contacts you.**

Even if they sound convincing.

</div>

---

## What about approval notifications?

Sometimes there is no code.

Your phone may simply ask:

> Are you trying to sign in?

with buttons such as:

**Approve**

or:

**Deny**

If you are not trying to log in:

## Choose Deny

---

## Repeated notifications

Sometimes attackers repeatedly send login requests hoping you will eventually press:

**Approve**

just to make the notifications stop.

This is sometimes called:

## MFA fatigue

or:

## push bombing

<div className="alert alert--info" role="alert">

If you keep receiving login approval requests that you did not start, do not approve them just to make them disappear.

</div>

---

## What should you do?

If you receive an unexpected security code or login request:

### 1. Deny the login

If there is a deny option, use it.

### 2. Do not share the code

Not by phone, message or email.

### 3. Check your account

Look for:

- unfamiliar logins
- unfamiliar devices
- security alerts

### 4. Consider changing your password

Especially if you think somebody may know it.

### 5. Check two-step verification

Make sure:

- your phone number is still correct
- your recovery details have not changed
- no unfamiliar authentication method was added

---

## A useful warning sign

An unexpected login notification can sometimes tell you something useful:

### Someone may already know your password.

Your second security step could be the thing stopping them.

<div className="alert alert--success" role="alert">

**Two-step verification is doing its job when it stops a login you did not start.**

Do not help the attacker past the second step.

</div>

---

## Quick scenario

You receive this message:

> Your security code is 739201. Do not share this code.

Two minutes later, somebody phones saying:

> "Hello, this is your bank. Can you confirm the code we just sent you?"

### What do you do?

**Do not give them the code.**

End the call and contact the organisation yourself using a trusted phone number or app.

---

## The rule to remember

<div className="alert alert--success" role="alert">

**If you did not start the login, do not approve the login.**

And never give someone your verification code just because they ask for it.

</div>
